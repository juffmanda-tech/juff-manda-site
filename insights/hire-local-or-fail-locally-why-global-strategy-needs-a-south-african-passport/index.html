Hire local or fail locally: why global strategy needs a South African passport.

Market strategy  ·  9 Aug 2026  ·  Juff Manda

There is a particular kind of confidence that shows up in a deck titled "Global Strategy, Localise as Needed." It is the confidence of someone who has never had to explain load shedding to a media planner, or why a billboard that works in Frankfurt will die a quiet death in the Northern Cape.

I was speaking recently to a friend at an international B2C brand actively trying to break into the South African market. No marketing department on the ground. No brand manager who has ever set foot here. The person steering strategy for South African consumers sits in another country entirely, building plans off research that rarely goes deeper than a market report and a few slides of cultural context. And this is not a new story to me. I have sat in that exact seat before, on the other side of a call, watching someone confidently present a strategy for a market they have never lived in.

The billboard test

Here is the simplest way I can put it. If a billboard works in Germany, that tells you nothing about whether it will work in South Africa. And that is not even the interesting part. A billboard that works in Sandton will not automatically work in the Northern Cape. That is how layered this market is.

South Africa has 12 official languages as of 2023, and English, the language most brands default to, is the home language of fewer than 10% of the population. Roughly 23% of South Africans speak Zulu as a home language. 16% speak Xhosa. 13% speak Afrikaans. McKinsey's research on winning in African consumer markets is direct about this: linguistic diversity alone means companies need to deliver their marketing messages in a plethora of languages, and that differences in consumer behaviour run far deeper than language. Even South Africans researching an unfamiliar province will do some homework. But most of the instinct is already there, absorbed simply by living here. That is the advantage a market like ours gives you when the people building the strategy are actually in it.

"Localise the strategy" is a warning sign

When a global team hands over a finished plan and says localise as needed, what they are really telling you is that they built something without understanding the market it needs to work in. It is not a starting point. It is a patch job waiting to happen.

The evidence is in the market entry failures. Shoprite spent nearly two decades and hundreds of millions of rand building what it called a pan-African retail empire, operating across 15 African countries and running 25 stores in Nigeria alone. By 2025, it had quietly exited Nigeria, Kenya, Uganda, Ghana, Malawi, the DRC, and Madagascar, retreating to the one market where it had always made money: home. Shoprite is not a small underfunded brand. It is one of Africa's most recognised retail names with deep operational infrastructure. And it still got it wrong in market after market, because the playbook it built for one market did not translate to another.

A strategy built for global and adjusted afterwards for South Africa is not a South African strategy. It is a global strategy wearing a costume. The methodology does not need to change. Good marketing principles are good marketing principles anywhere. What has to change is who is allowed to make the calls on insight, tone, language, and cultural reference, because that is where campaigns actually live or die.

Mntana Ka Gogo and the proof in the work

Vaseline's Heritage Day campaign is the case study I keep coming back to. The post, created by VML South Africa, centred on the deeply familiar South African ritual of a caregiver rubbing Vaseline vigorously into a child's skin before school, a daily act of love that generations of black South Africans carry into adulthood. The copy was written in vernac. The image was shot in 20 minutes and starred the daughter of one of the creatives.

It went viral across South Africa immediately. Then it crossed borders. People around the world who did not understand every word responded to the humanity of it. The campaign went on to win two Clios, a Silver Pencil at The One Show, and a Shortlist at D&AD. Kenosi Matsebatlela, Head of Marketing for Vaseline, put it plainly: "People are telling us that they feel seen."

Nobody built that campaign by starting with global and working backwards. Someone who understood the South African cultural moment built something true, and the rest followed. Industry voices called it a masterclass in showing up with culture, not in it. That is what local expertise buys you: content specific enough to be real, and only afterwards, sometimes, universal enough to travel.

Not a discrimination argument. A competence argument.

I want to be precise about what I am saying here, because it is easy to hear this as gatekeeping. It is not. It works exactly the same way in reverse. I have no business building a strategy for the American market. I do not live that culture, I do not carry its instincts, and no amount of surface-level research changes that.

Afrolingo's 2025 analysis of localisation in South Africa puts it well: success comes down to how intentionally you adapt your communication. Transcreation, not translation. Native speaker validation, not Google Translate. Cultural references that feel native, not borrowed. This is not about where someone is from. It is about whether the person making the calls actually understands the audience they are speaking to.

When international brands move into South Africa, particularly ones selling to the mass market, the message is simple: hire local. Not as a compliance checkbox, not as a token seat at the table, but as the people actually shaping the insight and the idea. That is where the impact comes from. Everything else is a strategy in a costume, waiting for someone who actually knows the market to make it real.
